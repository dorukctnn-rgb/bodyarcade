How webcam fitness games work, and how to set up your room

Updated 25 October 2026 · 6 min read

A webcam fitness game is a game you control by moving in front of an ordinary camera: the one in your laptop lid, on top of your monitor or on the front of your phone. There is no controller, no wristband and no console. The camera watches, software turns what it sees into a few body positions, and the game reacts to them the way it would react to a button press. This guide explains how that works in a browser, what it can and cannot read, and how to set up a room. We make BodyArcade, so we use it as the example; the advice applies to any game that uses a camera.

How a browser reads your body

Modern browsers can run small machine-learning models on your own device. The one most webcam games use for bodies is a pose landmarker. It takes a camera frame and returns the position of 33 points on your body, such as the nose, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees and ankles, as coordinates in the frame. BodyArcade uses Google’s MediaPipe Pose Landmarker. It runs in a background thread of the page where the browser allows it, on your graphics chip when the device has usable graphics acceleration and on the processor when it does not. The video frames are not sent anywhere.

A game does not need all 33 points. It picks a few stable ones and reads simple relationships between them:

  • Squat: your hips drop a long way below their standing height, then rise again (Squat Flap).
  • Knee lift: a knee comes up towards your hip, one leg after the other (Knee Sprint).
  • Jumping jack: arms and feet go out together, then come back (Firework Jacks).
  • Twist and side bend: your shoulders turn against your hips, or tilt sideways over them (Twist Turbine, Limbo Bend).
  • Reach and pull: a hand goes above its shoulder, then comes down towards it (Summit Climb).
  • Hit: a hand arrives at a target on the screen (Star Catch, Pad Work, Reaction Tag).
  • Shape: your body matches a pose before a wall reaches you (Pose Wall).

Measured against your own body

The camera does not know how tall you are or how far away you stand, so a good game measures everything against your own body. Before a game, BodyArcade asks you to stand where you will play and measures your height, your arm reach and a comfortable squat depth. A squat then means the same physical thing for a child and for a tall adult. Games that skip this step and use fixed pixel thresholds tend to work for the person who made them and for nobody else.

What one camera reads well, and what it does not

Reliable: large moves of large body parts. Squats, knee lifts, jumping jacks, twists, side bends, reaching overhead and stepping all survive a cheap camera, uneven light and a cluttered room.

Less reliable: fine hand positions and finger gestures, very fast moves, anything hidden behind a table or another person, and movement towards or away from the camera, because one camera has no real sense of depth.

A game built around what a camera sees well, with a small set of clear moves, beats one with twenty moves that register half the time. When a move does not count, a good game says why. BodyArcade lists a reason for every miss, for example “Hips not low enough” or “Your hands were out of view”, so you can fix the cause and not guess.

Set up a room that works the first time

  • Space. Clear about 2 metres around you and play on a floor that is not slippery.
  • Distance. For whole-body games your head to your ankles must be in view: stand 2 to 3 metres from a laptop or 1.5 to 2 metres from an upright phone. For upper-body games your head to your hips must be in view, about 1 metre away.
  • Camera height. Hip height works best. Stand a phone upright at hip height, leaning on something steady. Tilt a laptop screen back until the camera sees your feet, then step back 2.5 to 3 metres. A laptop on the floor, pointing up, distorts your proportions.
  • Light. Put the light in front of you or beside you, not behind you. A window behind you turns you into a silhouette and the model loses your joints.
  • People. Only the player should be in the picture, unless you play with two. A passer-by can confuse the tracking for a moment.
  • Clothes. Anything that contrasts with the wall behind you helps.
  • Phones. Sideways shows more of the room, so wide moves have space; upright gives a taller picture. BodyArcade asks the browser to keep the screen on during a round where the browser allows it. In other camera apps, turn off auto-lock so the screen does not dim in the middle of a round.

Seated, quiet and low impact

Not everyone can or wants to stand and jump. In BodyArcade every game has a seated version, and 10 of 11 can be played quietly, without jumping or stamping, which matters in a flat with neighbours below. At a desk the app notices when it only sees your head and shoulders and offers desk mode. A camera game that cannot be played seated shuts out many people.

Webcam games win on friction. There is nothing to buy and nothing to charge, and a round can start a minute after you decide to move. They lose on resistance, because a camera cannot make you push against anything, and on precision. If you want an easy way to raise your pulse in a living room, they fit. If you want a strength programme, look at a console title or a weights app, or use a webcam game as the warm-up.

What to look for in a webcam fitness game

  • On-device processing. The video should be analysed on your device and never uploaded. Read the privacy policy; if it does not say, assume it is uploaded.
  • A calibration step. A short “stand here” check means the game measures you instead of guessing.
  • Feedback you can see. A small preview with your skeleton drawn over it tells you at once whether the camera has lost you.
  • Reasons for misses. A game that says why a move did not count lets you improve.
  • Short rounds. Pose games tire you in a good way. A few minutes is enough before your form drops.
  • Seated and quiet versions. They let the whole family and the neighbours join in.
  • A way to play without the camera. Keyboard or touch controls let you try a game on a train.
